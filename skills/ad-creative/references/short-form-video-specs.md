# Short-form vertical video: filming spec and creator formats

What anyone filming a vertical video on a phone needs to know for Reels, Stories, TikTok or Shorts: the frame, the safe zones, captions and sound. Part 2 is a library of creator and founder formats to use as filming ideas.

Part 1 applies to every vertical video. Part 2 is a menu of structures, not scripts. For the wider list of Meta ad formats, see [meta-creative-formats.md](meta-creative-formats.md).

---

## Part 1: The vertical video spec

### Canvas
- Film and export in 9:16 (portrait). Meta recommends 1440 x 2560 pixels for Reels and Stories.
- Shoot on a phone held upright. Footage of any resolution is fine as long as it fills the portrait frame.
- A free phone editor is enough for cutting, captions and music. You do not need paid software.

### Safe zones
The app's own buttons and text sit on top of your video: the account name, the caption, the music label and the like and share buttons. Anything you place under them gets covered. Meta's published guidance for Reels and Stories is to keep these parts of the frame free of text and logos:

- about 14% at the top
- about 35% at the bottom
- about 6% on each side

Put captions and anything the viewer must read inside the middle of the frame. Other platforms (TikTok, YouTube Shorts) publish their own safe zones, and they differ from Meta's, so check each platform's own spec page before you reuse a video there. Apps change their layout from time to time, so re-check before a large push.

### Caption style
Most people watch with the sound off, so captions carry the message.

- Large enough to read on a small phone screen without zooming.
- High contrast: white text with a dark outline or shadow works on almost any footage. A solid dark box behind the text also works.
- Short lines, a few words at a time, never a block of text.
- Keep captions inside the safe zones above.
- Show each caption for the whole of the clip it belongs to, so the viewer is never waiting for text to appear.
- Use the same font and size throughout one video.

### Audio
- Assume the viewer has the sound off, so the video must make sense with captions alone.
- Music must be licensed, or taken from the platform's own library of commercial-use sounds. A popular chart track is usually not cleared for an ad, even if it is available for ordinary posts. Check the library's terms for business accounts.
- If a person speaks, record in a quiet room, close to the phone, and keep their voice louder than any music.
- Fade music out over the last second or so. A sudden cut to silence feels like a mistake.
- For an organic post (not a paid ad), many creators add a sound inside the app after uploading, so others can reuse it. For a paid ad, the sound is usually part of the video file. Check what the platform allows for ads.

## Part 2: Creator format library

These are filming ideas used by creators and brands on short-form video. Each is a structure to fill with your own footage and your own words.

Some terms used below:
- UGC (user-generated content): video in the style of an ordinary person, filmed on a phone, rather than a studio production. In ads it is usually made by a creator you have agreed terms with.
- Hard cut: a straight switch from one clip to the next with no fade or effect.
- Greenscreen: an editing effect that removes the background behind a person so they appear in front of other footage or images.
- Yapper: slang for a creator who talks to the camera at length, in a chatty personal style.
- VSL (video sales letter): a longer video that explains a problem and a solution step by step, then makes an offer.

The tier letters (S, A, B, C) come from one practitioner's ranking, Dara Denney's tier list of Meta creative types. They are her opinion for brands with creator budgets, not a platform fact and not Meta's data. Roughly, S and A are formats she says can reach new audiences, B are formats she says mainly convert people who already know the brand, and C are formats that need rights, special talent or extra care. A small account does not need all of them. Start with one or two you can film yourself, and judge them by the one stop rule in `paid-ads-strategy`.

Any format that shows a customer, a review or a result must show a real one. Never film or caption something that did not happen.

### Format 1: Reaction and demo (hard cut) · tier A
Shape: a short reaction clip with a hook caption, then a hard cut to a screen recording or product demo. About 9 to 12 seconds in all.

```
[ reaction, about 3 seconds, hook caption ] then [ demo, with an optional caption on the result ]
```

- When to use it: you have a genuine reaction from a real person and a clear demo.
- The hook caption sits on the reaction clip and does the selling. Write it as what the person was thinking ("I was about to give up on this and then I tried it"), not as a product claim.
- The hard cut is the mechanic: the reaction gets attention, the cut delivers the payoff.
- The reaction must be real. The format is only as good as the honesty of the person on screen.

### Format 2: "No yapping" split-screen tutorial · tier B
Shape: a silent, quick tutorial. A full-screen intro, then a screen split in half: the action on one half, the result on the other. Step captions sit at the line between them.

```
[ intro, full screen, hook ] then [ split: input on one side, result on the other, numbered step captions ]
```

- When to use it: a how-to where showing is better than telling, such as a set-up flow or a tool walkthrough.
- Captions carry the steps, one per beat, placed at the split so both halves stay visible.
- No voiceover. Keep the pace tight; dead air loses viewers.

### Format 3: Greenscreen reaction · tier A
Shape: a video plays full screen and the creator, cut out from their own background, appears over it to react or comment. They can start large in the middle and then shrink into a corner.

```
[ full-screen video or screen recording, with the creator cut out and laid on top ]
```

- When to use it: reacting to a trend, a screen recording or your own product.
- Both sound tracks can play (the underlying video and the creator). The creator's voice is the point.
- Only react to content you own or have permission to use. For someone else's video, see Format 11.

### Format 4: Yapper · tier A
Shape: one person talks straight to camera and tells a personal story that ends with your product. No cuts are needed. About 20 to 60 seconds.

```
[ person talking to camera, hook line first, personal story, product as the turning point ]
```

- When to use it: you have the right person and a story with a real beginning, middle and end.
- Open on a strong statement or story hook ("I nearly cancelled this three times"), not a product claim. The product should enter as the turn in the story, not as a list of features.
- Captions on, an everyday setting (a car, a walk, a kitchen). Flat energy kills this format, so casting matters more than the script.
- The story must be true to the speaker's real experience.

### Format 5: Amateur investigation · tier A
Shape: a creator looks into a question on the viewer's behalf: visiting places, comparing options, testing claims. The search itself keeps people watching.

```
[ creator asks the question, goes and looks into it on camera, ends on your product as what they found ]
```

- When to use it: your product holds up when compared or checked.
- Pick a real question ("are extended warranties worth it?"), let the creator do the legwork on camera, and let the product appear as the finding, not as a sponsor slot.
- Real footage of real places and comparisons is what makes it believable. Do not stage a result.

### Format 6: David and Goliath · tier A
Shape: the brand is the underdog against a big incumbent, an industry habit or a broken norm.

```
[ name the Goliath, show the brand's fight against it, say why you are different ]
```

- When to use it: there is a real problem with how the category treats people, and you can say plainly how you do it differently.
- Make the problem concrete and the stakes human. The brand's origin ("we started this because X was broken") drives it. Works well with a founder on camera.
- Do not invent a villain. Keep comparisons about other companies factual and provable.

### Format 7: Authority · tier A
Shape: a credentialed expert, such as a doctor, an engineer or a practitioner, explains the problem and why a product is the right answer.

```
[ expert on camera with credentials clear, the problem in their field, why this product fits ]
```

- When to use it: trust-heavy categories such as health, skincare or finance.
- The expert must be real, the credentials must be real, and every claim must be true and provable. Never put words in an expert's mouth or imply an endorsement they have not given.
- Health, medical and financial claims carry the highest risk. Run them through `ad-claims-check` before filming and before launch.

### Format 8: VSL (video sales letter) · tier S
Shape: a longer video (from about a minute to several minutes) that teaches before it sells: the problem, why it happens, the solution with proof, then the offer.

```
[ hook and problem, why it happens, the solution with proof, the offer and a call to action ]
```

- When to use it: the buyer has to understand how something works before they will buy.
- The skill is in the script: a tight opening, an explanation people can believe, honest proof, and a clear offer. Keep viewers watching by adding a new fact every few lines.
- Captions throughout. A real person on camera, or a voiceover over your own footage, both work.
- Proof must be real: real customers, real results, real numbers from your own records.

### Format 9: Greenscreen commentary · tier A
Shape: the creator talks over full-frame images, such as screenshots, product photos, charts or a web page, and explains each one as it appears. This is a teaching format, unlike the reaction in Format 3.

```
[ creator cut out, full-frame images behind them, narration matched to what is on screen ]
```

- When to use it: anything where showing the thing while explaining it beats talking alone.
- Change the background image at each point in the narration so the picture always matches what is being said.
- Keep it useful. If it reads as a disguised pitch, viewers leave.

### Format 10: Conversation · tier B
Shape: two people in a real exchange, such as an interview or a back-and-forth, where the product comes up naturally.

```
[ two people talking, a real question and answer, product enters as part of the talk ]
```

- When to use it: the product fits a natural conversational moment and the two people have real chemistry.
- Hard to do well. Scripted-sounding dialogue ruins it. It works best when one person raises a real objection and the other answers it.

### Format 11: Duet or reaction to another creator's video · tier C
Shape: react to, duet or stitch another creator's video, with your commentary alongside or after their clip.

```
[ the original creator's clip, then your reaction responding to it ]
```

- When to use it: there is a specific post worth responding to.
- You need the original creator's permission before using their footage, and for a paid ad you need permission to use it in an ad. Without clear permission, do not run it as an ad.

### Format 12: ASMR · tier C
Shape: sensory, sound-led video such as tapping, unboxing, applying or textures, with the product as the object. ASMR stands for autonomous sensory meridian response: the calm, tingling feeling some people get from certain quiet sounds.

```
[ close-up sensory action, product in front, the sound carries the video ]
```

- When to use it: pet, beauty, food or tactile products where the sensory experience is the appeal.
- This breaks the rule that most people watch muted, so it works only for viewers with sound on. Record the audio cleanly.
- It needs someone who really makes this kind of video.

### Format 13: Street interview · tier C
Shape: questions put to people in a public place, capturing their honest reactions to your product or a question about your category.

```
[ street setting, question to passers-by, candid answers, then your angle ]
```

- When to use it: you want the credibility of unscripted public reaction.
- Use real people only, with their consent, and keep their real answers. Get their agreement (ideally written) before you use their face or words in an ad, and say so if they ask to be left out.
- Never stage fake customers, hire actors to play passers-by, or recreate "interviews" and present them as real. If real capture is too hard, choose another format.

### Founder and organic vlog structures

For founder-led video and brand content that feels native to the platform, here are four story shapes. They are not a separate tier. They are the story inside a yapper video, an investigation or a vlog. A founder telling why they started the business is often a brand's first strong video, because people with the same problem recognise themselves in it.

The four structures (pick one, then film to it):
- Hero's journey: take what is happening in the business and tell it as a story: the problem, the backstory, the attempt, the setback, the realisation, the breakthrough, and what comes next. The way you frame the events matters more than the events. One good story-style video a week can beat daily posting because people come back to follow the story.
- Money lens: tell a story through cost, such as a breakdown or a fixed-budget challenge ("I spent £200 on Meta ads, here is where it went"). Surprisingly cheap results interest people more than expensive ones. Do not use luxury as the hook.
- Shiny object: build the video around something visually new that you have real access to, such as your workshop, a machine, a craft process or a trade show.
- Niche guide with expertise: describe the world through your professional eye ("what I would avoid as an interior designer", filmed in a shop). A learner's view also works if you are honest about which you are.

A simple filming system that makes any of these faster:
- Film each moment three ways: close, medium and wide (the 0.5x lens on many phones).
- Keep clips short, 2 to 3 seconds. Many short clips are easier to edit than one long walk-around.
- If the subject is moving, hold the phone still. If nothing is moving, add a slow push in or side slide.
- Do the activity first, then spend about five minutes at the end filming three angles of 10 to 12 things, so you interrupt less.
- Keep one phone folder per shoot and mark your best opening shot as a favourite. Film at least five shots of yourself, since you are the thread through the video.

The edit: aim for each shot to last half a second to one second. Record the voiceover or talking track first, lay clips under it, then reorder and trim. A free phone editor is enough. This fast cut rhythm is the same idea as the hard cut in Format 1, and it stops footage feeling slow.
